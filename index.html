PSA testing for prostate cancer: benefits and risks

Information and guidance for patients and carers in England

A PSA test measures a protein called prostate-specific antigen in your blood. A raised PSA can be a sign of prostate cancer, but it can also be caused by something else, such as an enlarged prostate or a urine infection. The test cannot diagnose or rule out prostate cancer on its own. (NHS: PSA test; GOV.UK: Prostate cancer screening evidence)

England does not routinely invite everyone at a certain age for a PSA test. That does not mean you cannot have one. If you have symptoms or are worried about your risk, you can talk to your GP about whether testing is right for you. (UK NSC: Prostate cancer screening recommendation; NHS: How to get a PSA test)

What are the benefits and risks?

Potential benefits

  • It can help find cancer before symptoms appear. A raised result can be a reason to look more closely. (NHS Northern Cancer Alliance: PSA testing decision guide)
  • Finding a fast-growing cancer early can make a difference. It may be easier to treat before it spreads. For some people, earlier diagnosis and treatment may help them live longer. (GOV.UK: Benefits and harms of screening)

Potential risks

  • A raised result does not always mean cancer. It may cause worry and lead to more tests. If a biopsy is needed, it can cause pain, bleeding or infection. (GOV.UK: Follow-up tests and their risks)
  • A normal result can miss cancer. Do not ignore new or ongoing symptoms because an earlier PSA result was normal. (GOV.UK: Limits of PSA testing; NHS: Prostate cancer symptoms)
  • It can find a slow-growing cancer that would never have caused you harm. This is called overdiagnosis. Some people then have treatment they did not need, which can cause lasting problems such as leaking urine, difficulty getting or keeping an erection, or bowel problems. (UK NSC: Harms of screening; GOV.UK: Treatment side effects)

Why isn’t mass screening recommended in England?

Mass screening would mean routinely inviting people without symptoms for testing based on age, even if they have not asked for a test. The UK National Screening Committee (UK NSC) concluded that this is more likely to cause harm than good across the general population. Although screening can find some serious cancers early, it would also lead to many people being diagnosed with slow-growing cancer or having tests or treatment they did not need. An MRI scan can help avoid some unnecessary biopsies, but it does not remove the risk of overdiagnosis. (UK NSC: Current recommendation; GOV.UK: Why population screening is not recommended)

There is one targeted recommendation. The UK NSC recommends a PSA test every two years for men aged 45 to 61 who have both a confirmed harmful inherited change in the BRCA2 gene and a family history of breast, ovarian, pancreatic or prostate cancer. Prostate cancer is more likely to occur earlier and be aggressive in this group, so the committee judged that the benefits outweigh the harms. England has accepted this recommendation and plans to introduce the programme in 2027. (UK NSC: Who targeted screening is recommended for; GOV.UK: Why this group is eligible; GOV.UK: England’s implementation update)

No other group is currently recommended for routine invitations. Black men and people with a family history of prostate cancer can be at higher risk, but higher risk alone does not establish that a screening programme will do more good than harm. Research is ongoing, including the TRANSFORM trial. You can still discuss your own risk and whether to have a test with your GP. (UK NSC: Other higher-risk groups; GOV.UK: TRANSFORM research; NHS: Asking about a PSA test)

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I ask for a PSA test if I feel well?

Yes. Men and anyone with a prostate can ask their GP about testing, even if they feel well. You can discuss your age, family history and other risk factors, what a result could mean for you, and whether you would want further tests if the result were raised. (NHS: How to get a PSA test; GOV.UK: Risk factors and informed choice)

What happens if my PSA is raised?

Your GP may repeat the blood test or refer you to a specialist. An MRI scan is often used before deciding whether you need a biopsy. A biopsy means taking small samples of prostate tissue to check for cancer. A raised PSA does not mean you automatically need a biopsy. (NHS: Getting your results; GOV.UK: MRI and biopsy)

Is testing because of symptoms the same as screening?

No. Screening looks for disease in people without symptoms. If you have symptoms, a PSA test may be part of finding out what is causing them. The recommendation against mass screening does not mean you should wait to have symptoms checked. (GOV.UK: What screening means; NHS: Symptoms and assessment)

When to ask for help

Contact your GP practice if you notice changes such as needing to pee more often, a weak flow, trouble starting to pee, or blood in your pee or semen. These symptoms often have causes other than cancer, but they are worth checking even if you have previously had a normal PSA result. (NHS: Prostate cancer symptoms; GOV.UK: Limits of PSA testing)

If you know you carry a harmful BRCA2 variant, or you are worried about a family history of prostate or related cancers, mention it when you speak to your GP or genetics team. You can ask what the guidance means for you now rather than waiting for a screening invitation. (UK NSC: Targeted screening criteria; NHS: Discussing a PSA test)
